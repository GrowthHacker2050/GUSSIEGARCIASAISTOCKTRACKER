    The basics

    Plain-English rules most long-term investors follow.

    1. Start with a core index fund

    An S&P 500 fund like VOO owns 500 companies at once, including Nvidia, Microsoft and Tesla. If one company stumbles, the others carry it. Over the past 10 years VOO grew about – a year.

    Many people keep half or more of their money in a core fund and use the rest for individual growth stocks.

    2. Don't let one stock be most of your money

    A common guideline is to keep any single stock under about 5–10% of your total. Tesla made you a great gain, but if it fell 65% like it did in 2022, that would hit most of your savings at once.

    Ways people reduce it: put new money and the Roth IRA into other investments, or sell Tesla a little at a time.

    3. Roth IRA vs. a regular account

    Roth IRA: buy and sell inside it with no tax, and money taken out after age 59½ (account open 5+ years) is tax-free. It's a great place for the highest-growth holdings. 2026 limit: $7,500 a year, or $8,600 at age 50+.

    Regular account (where Tesla likely is): selling at a profit means capital gains tax on the gain. Held over a year, the federal rate is 0%, 15% or 20% depending on income. Ask a tax professional before selling a big gain.

    4. How long to hold

    Index funds: 10+ years; ignore the dips.
    Big established companies (risk 2–3): 5+ years.
    High-growth stocks (risk 4–5): only money you won't need for 10 years and could watch drop 50–80% without selling.
    Crypto: keep it small; it has fallen 50–80% several times.

    5. Once a year, rebalance

    Pick a date (like your birthday). If one holding grew far past its target %, sell a little and add to the ones that fell behind. This locks in gains and keeps risk in check, without watching the market every day.
